Keyword Research for SEO: A Practical 10-Step Guide

Learn how to find the right keywords, understand what your audience is searching for and turn your research into a focused SEO content strategy.

By Alioth Digital Studios Published: 17 Nov 2026 Last updated: 17 Nov 2026 8 min read

Keyword research is the process of discovering and analysing the words and phrases people use when searching online. It helps you understand what your audience needs, how they describe their problems and which topics your website should address.

However, effective keyword research involves more than finding phrases with high search volumes. You need to consider search intent, competition, relevance and the value a search could bring to your business. A keyword is only useful when your page can provide the answer or solution the searcher expects.

This guide explains how to do keyword research for SEO step by step. You'll learn how to find keyword ideas, use research tools, analyse competing pages, organise keyword groups and turn your findings into a practical content plan.

What is keyword research in SEO?

Keyword research in SEO is the process of finding and analysing search queries that are relevant to your website, business or audience. It reveals the language people use, the topics they care about and the type of information they expect to find.

For example, someone searching for “how to improve website speed” is likely looking for instructions, while someone searching for “website speed optimisation service” may be comparing providers. These phrases relate to a similar topic, but the search intent is different.

Understanding this difference helps you create the right page for the right audience at the right stage of their decision-making process.

Why is keyword research important?

Keyword research gives your SEO strategy direction. Without it, you may publish content that does not match what people are searching for or target phrases that are too broad, too competitive or unrelated to your services.

  • Understand your audience: discover the questions, problems and language used by potential customers.
  • Create relevant content: choose topics based on genuine search demand and user needs.
  • Match search intent: build pages that provide the type of result searchers expect.
  • Find realistic opportunities: identify specific and long-tail keywords that may be more achievable.
  • Organise your website: group related topics and map them to the most appropriate pages.
  • Measure and improve: use search performance data to refine existing content and identify new opportunities.

How to do keyword research for SEO: 10 practical steps

1. Understand your business and audience

Start by defining what your website offers, who it serves and what problems it solves. List your main products, services, topics and customer questions. Think about the language a potential customer would naturally use—not only the industry terminology your team uses internally.

2. Build a list of seed keywords

Seed keywords are broad terms that describe your core topics. If your website offers web development, examples might include “web development”, “business website”, “ecommerce website” and “website redesign”. Use these as starting points for discovering more specific searches.

3. Use keyword research tools

Keyword tools can expand your seed list and provide useful estimates or related ideas. Consider Google Keyword Planner for keyword ideas and advertising data, Google Trends for changes in interest over time, and platforms such as Ahrefs and Semrush for keyword and competitor research. Google Search Console is useful for finding queries that already bring impressions or clicks to your website. SEO Miner is a free workspace for extracting page keywords and comparing competitor pages. Treat tool metrics as estimates and validate important opportunities against actual search results.

4. Evaluate search volume and competition

Search volume estimates how often a keyword is searched, while keyword difficulty or competition indicates how challenging it may be to rank. These metrics are useful for comparison, but they should not determine your decision alone. A lower-volume phrase with clear relevance and strong intent may be more valuable than a broad, high-volume keyword that attracts the wrong visitors.

5. Identify search intent

Search intent is the reason behind a query. Common categories include informational (learning something), navigational (finding a particular site or page), commercial investigation (researching options) and transactional (taking an action or making a purchase). Search your target phrase and review the results. The page types, formats and topics ranking on the first page provide clues about what searchers expect.

6. Find long-tail keywords

Long-tail keywords are more specific search phrases, often containing several words. Examples include “keyword research for a new ecommerce website” or “how to find low competition SEO keywords”. They may have lower search volume, but can reveal a clearer need and help you create focused content. Use question-based searches, autocomplete suggestions and related searches to find them.

7. Analyse competitor keywords

Identify websites that compete for the same audience or topics in search results. Review the pages ranking for your target terms and look for subjects they cover, questions they answer and keyword variations they target. Competitor research should reveal gaps and ideas—not encourage you to copy another website. Check whether the topic is relevant to your own expertise and audience before adding it to your plan.

8. Group keywords into topic clusters

Organise related keywords into groups based on shared meaning and search intent. For example, “keyword research process”, “how to research SEO keywords” and “keyword research steps” may belong to one topic cluster. Grouping helps prevent several pages from competing for the same query and makes it easier to plan comprehensive resources.

9. Prioritise your keyword opportunities

Choose keywords by considering relevance, intent, competition, search demand and business value together. A simple prioritisation approach is to ask: Is this topic relevant to our audience? Can our website provide a genuinely useful answer? Is the current search results page achievable for us? Does the topic support a meaningful business objective?

10. Map keywords to the right pages

Assign each primary keyword or tightly related keyword group to the most appropriate page. Use informational keywords for guides and articles, service-focused phrases for service pages, and product or solution terms for relevant landing pages. Give each page a clear primary topic, then incorporate related terms naturally where they help explain the subject.

Keyword research example: From search term to content plan

Let's look at a practical example of how a service-based business could research keywords and organise them into useful website pages.

Imagine a UK business offering professional bid writing and tender management services. Rather than targeting one broad phrase, the business could start with a seed topic such as "bid writing".

Illustrative keyword research and page mapping example
Keyword idea Likely search intent Suggested page
What is bid writing? Informational Educational blog article
How to write a tender response Informational Step-by-step guide
Professional bid writing services Commercial investigation Bid writing service page
Tender management support Commercial investigation Tender management service page
How to improve a tender submission Informational Practical advice article

This is an illustrative example, not a verified list of search volumes or keyword difficulty scores. You should validate each phrase using keyword tools and the actual search results before deciding which pages to create.

How to turn the example into a keyword strategy

  1. Start with the main topic: use "bid writing" as the seed topic and identify related services and customer questions.
  2. Expand the keyword list: use keyword tools, customer conversations and Google search suggestions to discover variations.
  3. Check search intent: determine whether people want an explanation, practical instructions or a service provider.
  4. Group related phrases: organise keywords that share a topic and search intent into clusters.
  5. Map each cluster: assign each group to an appropriate existing page or a new content opportunity.
  6. Review for overlap: check that multiple pages are not unnecessarily targeting the same search intent.

The result should be a structured website where informational articles support relevant service pages, helping visitors move naturally from learning about a problem to exploring a potential solution.

How to use Google Search Console for keyword research

Google Search Console is particularly useful because it shows the queries for which your website has already appeared in Google Search. Instead of relying entirely on estimated keyword data, you can examine actual impressions and clicks associated with your pages.

Follow these steps

  1. Open the Performance report: select Search results in Google Search Console.
  2. Choose a suitable date range: compare recent performance with an earlier period where useful.
  3. Review the Queries tab: identify the searches generating impressions and clicks.
  4. Find relevant opportunities: look for queries that closely match your services or article topics.
  5. Check the Pages tab: identify which URL Google associates with those queries.
  6. Inspect the actual page: check whether its title, headings and content answer the searcher's question.
  7. Monitor changes: compare impressions, clicks, click-through rate and average position after making improvements.

For example, if an existing article receives impressions for a relevant question that it only mentions briefly, you could improve that section with a clearer explanation or practical example. Avoid adding content simply to include more keywords; the improvement should genuinely help the reader.

Remember that average position is an aggregated metric and can vary by query, location, device and date range. Review individual queries and pages before deciding what to change.

You can access Google Search Console to review your website's search performance.

How to choose the right keywords

Once you have collected keyword ideas, the next step is deciding which ones deserve attention. The best keywords are not necessarily those with the highest search volume. Prioritise phrases that are relevant to your audience and match the purpose of the page you intend to create.

Use the following criteria to assess each opportunity:

  • Relevance: does the search relate directly to your business, audience or subject expertise?
  • Search intent: can your proposed page satisfy what the searcher is trying to achieve?
  • Competition: are the pages currently ranking realistic competitors, and can you produce something genuinely more useful?
  • Search demand: is there evidence that people search for this topic? Treat volume estimates as directional rather than exact figures.
  • Business value: could attracting the right visitor support a meaningful business objective?
  • Content fit: does the keyword belong on an existing page, or would it justify a separate resource?

Use a simple keyword prioritisation framework

You can compare opportunities using a straightforward high, medium or low assessment. This helps organise your research without pretending that an arbitrary score can predict rankings.

Example keyword prioritisation framework
Factor High priority Lower priority
Relevance Closely matches your audience and services Only loosely related to your offering
Intent Matches the purpose of your planned page Requires a different type of content
Competition There is a realistic opportunity to create a stronger result Current results are difficult to compete with and offer no clear content gap
Business value Attracts visitors relevant to your objectives Likely to attract an unrelated audience

For a smaller or newer website, focusing on a few closely related and achievable topics can be more useful than trying to compete immediately for broad, highly competitive phrases.

Common keyword research mistakes to avoid

  • Choosing keywords based only on search volume.
  • Ignoring search intent and publishing the wrong type of page.
  • Targeting several unrelated topics on one page.
  • Creating multiple pages that compete for the same keyword.
  • Copying competitor keyword lists without checking relevance.
  • Forcing keywords into headings or paragraphs unnaturally.
  • Treating keyword difficulty and traffic estimates as guaranteed outcomes.
  • Failing to revisit keyword performance as search behaviour changes.

How to turn keyword research into an SEO content strategy

Keyword research becomes valuable when it informs what you publish and how your website is organised. Once you have a prioritised list, turn it into a content plan.

  1. Create topic groups: organise related keywords around the main subjects your audience cares about.
  2. Choose the right page type: decide whether each group belongs on a service page, landing page, blog article or supporting resource.
  3. Set a primary topic: give each page a clear focus and use related phrases naturally.
  4. Build internal links: connect relevant articles and service pages so readers and search engines can discover related content.
  5. Create genuinely useful content: answer the query clearly, provide practical detail and demonstrate relevant experience.
  6. Review performance: use Search Console and analytics to monitor impressions, clicks and engagement, then improve pages where there is a clear opportunity.

A well-planned content strategy should serve readers first. Keywords help you understand what to cover, but usefulness, clarity, accuracy and a good page experience are what make the content worth visiting.

Frequently asked questions about keyword research

What is keyword research in SEO?

Keyword research is the process of finding and analysing the search terms people use in search engines. It helps you understand audience needs, identify relevant topics and create pages that match search intent.

How do beginners do keyword research?

Start with your main business topics and customer questions. Expand your ideas using keyword research tools, review the search results, assess relevance and intent, group related terms and map them to suitable pages.

Which keyword research tool is best?

There is no single best tool for every website. Google Keyword Planner, Google Trends and Google Search Console offer useful starting points. Ahrefs and Semrush provide additional keyword and competitor research features. Choose tools based on your requirements and budget.

Are long-tail keywords better for SEO?

Long-tail keywords can be useful because they are more specific and often reveal clearer search intent. However, their value depends on relevance, competition and whether your content can satisfy the searcher's needs.

How many keywords should I target on one page?

Focus on one primary topic and a closely related group of supporting terms. There is no fixed keyword count. Your page should cover the subject naturally without forcing repetitive phrases into the content.

What is keyword difficulty?

Keyword difficulty is an SEO tool metric that estimates how challenging it may be to rank for a search term. Different tools use different calculations, so treat it as a comparative indicator rather than a guarantee of ranking potential.

What is the difference between keyword research and keyword analysis?

Keyword research involves discovering search terms and questions used by your audience. Keyword analysis evaluates those terms by considering search intent, relevance, competition, demand and business value to decide which opportunities to prioritise.

How often should I do keyword research?

Review keyword opportunities when planning new content and revisit important pages periodically. Reassess sooner when your services, audience, competitors or search performance change.

Final thoughts

Keyword research is not just about finding popular search terms. It is about understanding your audience, identifying the intent behind their searches and creating pages that provide the most relevant answer.

By combining audience research, keyword tools, competitor analysis and thoughtful content planning, you can build an SEO strategy around realistic opportunities and useful information. Start with a focused list, prioritise relevance over volume and refine your approach as you learn what your audience responds to.
