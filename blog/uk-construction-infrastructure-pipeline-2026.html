UK Infrastructure Pipeline: 2026 Update

The March 2026 UK Infrastructure Pipeline identifies 734 planned projects representing £718 billion of public and private investment over the next decade. Here is what the pipeline means for contractors, civil engineers and specialist suppliers—and how to prepare for relevant opportunities.

By Alioth Digital Studios Published: 26 Nov 2026 Last updated: 26 Nov 2026 10–12 min read

The latest UK Infrastructure Pipeline update identifies 734 planned projects representing £718 billion of public and private investment over the next decade. Published by the National Infrastructure and Service Transformation Authority (NISTA) on 9 March 2026, the update gives businesses a clearer view of the scale and distribution of planned infrastructure activity.

For construction companies, civil engineering contractors, technical consultancies and specialist suppliers, the pipeline is a useful forward-looking source of market intelligence. It is not a list of guaranteed contracts or confirmed tender opportunities. Individual projects will progress at different speeds, and their procurement routes, funding, scope and delivery dates may change.

The commercial task for suppliers is therefore to use pipeline information to identify relevant markets, monitor upcoming procurement activity and prepare credible evidence of delivery capability before opportunities are formally advertised.

Strategic takeaway: Treat the pipeline as an early-warning and market-planning resource—not as guaranteed revenue. Build bid readiness around the projects, buyers and procurement routes that genuinely match your organisation’s capabilities.

1. The 2026 UK Infrastructure Pipeline: What the figures tell us

NISTA’s March 2026 update reports 734 planned projects and £718 billion of public and private investment over the next decade. It also provides additional analysis of future workforce and skills demand, helping industry understand the capacity required to deliver the planned programme.

The government estimates that delivering the projects in the pipeline will require an average annual construction and infrastructure workforce of between 629,000 and 706,000 over the next five years. This is a national estimate of workforce demand, not a guarantee that every role or project will proceed as currently planned.

The pipeline covers major economic and social infrastructure activity, including areas such as:

  • Energy generation, networks and decarbonisation infrastructure.
  • Road, rail and wider transport infrastructure.
  • Water, wastewater and flood-resilience investment.
  • Education and other public-sector estate projects.
  • Housing-related infrastructure and regeneration.

The published headline totals should be used with care. Sector totals can depend on the dataset’s definitions, project inclusion rules and treatment of public and private investment. Avoid adding together figures taken from different publications or presenting estimates as a definitive sector allocation unless they can be traced to the same NISTA dataset and methodology.

For suppliers, the most useful next step is to filter the pipeline by geography, sector, project stage, delivery period and likely buyer. A national headline figure is useful context; a shortlist of relevant projects is what supports a practical business-development plan.

2. How infrastructure work is procured: frameworks, dynamic markets and competitions

There is no single procurement route for the UK infrastructure pipeline. Contracting authorities and project sponsors use a range of approaches depending on the project, buyer, funding model, contract value, market conditions and applicable procurement rules.

Common routes to market

  • Framework agreements: These establish terms for awarding future contracts to one or more appointed suppliers. Depending on the framework rules, work may be awarded through direct award or a further competition.
  • Dynamic markets: Under the Procurement Act 2023, dynamic markets are lists of qualified suppliers that must remain open to new suppliers joining during their operation. They can be divided into categories or parts and used for goods, services and works.
  • Project-specific competitions: Some major projects use a bespoke competitive procedure or other project-specific procurement approach rather than relying on a general framework.
  • Supply-chain opportunities: Tier 1 contractors and other prime suppliers may procure specialist packages from subcontractors and suppliers. The process and visibility of these opportunities depend on the main contract, procurement documents and the buyer’s supply-chain arrangements.

Frameworks can provide an efficient route to repeat purchasing, but it is inaccurate to suggest that the overwhelming majority of all infrastructure capital spending necessarily flows through pre-competed frameworks. Suppliers should check the actual tender notice, framework rules and contracting authority requirements for each opportunity.

Where SMEs and specialist suppliers should focus

Smaller suppliers can participate as framework members, consortium partners, subcontractors or specialist providers. Useful preparation includes identifying relevant Tier 1 contractors, monitoring buyer and framework notices, maintaining current compliance documentation and developing concise evidence of previous delivery.

Do not assume that a fixed percentage of every public contract must be subcontracted to local SMEs. Any SME, local-supply-chain or social-value commitments should be checked against the specific procurement documents and contract conditions.

3. The Procurement Act 2023: understanding Most Advantageous Tender (MAT)

The Procurement Act 2023 came into force for new procurements on 24 February 2025. It introduced the concept of the Most Advantageous Tender (MAT), replacing the previous statutory terminology of the Most Economically Advantageous Tender (MEAT) for procurements governed by the new regime.

MAT gives contracting authorities flexibility under section 23 of the Act to assess tenders against award criteria that are relevant to the subject matter of the contract, sufficiently clear, measurable and specific, and proportionate to the nature, complexity and cost of the contract. Authorities must explain their assessment methodology and the relative importance of the criteria in the tender documents.

For construction and infrastructure suppliers, this means the tender response should address the published evaluation criteria—not rely on a generic claim that the lowest price or a broad promise of quality will be enough. Depending on the procurement, evaluation may consider matters such as:

  • The quality, safety and technical suitability of the proposed delivery method.
  • Programme, mobilisation, risk management and supply-chain resilience.
  • Whole-life performance, maintainability and relevant environmental impacts.
  • Relevant social value outcomes and measurable commitments where included in the procurement.
  • Price, cost, commercial assumptions and value for money.

Abnormally low tenders

A contracting authority may disregard a tender if it considers the price abnormally low for performing the contract. However, under Cabinet Office guidance on abnormally low tenders, it cannot reject the tender on that ground without first notifying the supplier and giving it a reasonable opportunity to demonstrate that it can perform the contract at the price offered. If the supplier provides a satisfactory justification, the authority may not disregard the tender as abnormally low.

For bidders, the practical lesson is to make pricing assumptions transparent and support them with a coherent cost model, resource plan, supply-chain quotations and clear explanations of efficiencies. A low price should be commercially defensible and deliverable.

Supplier performance and exclusion

The Act also contains provisions relating to excluded and excludable suppliers and the debarment list. Contracting authorities must apply the relevant statutory rules and procurement documents. Suppliers should maintain accurate performance records, resolve contractual issues promptly and ensure that any declarations requested during a procurement are complete and correct.

4. Social value and carbon reduction: what bidders need to know

Social value and carbon requirements can be important parts of public-sector construction tenders, but the applicable policy depends on the contracting authority, procurement route, contract value, timing and scope. They should not be treated as universal requirements applying identically to every public or private infrastructure contract.

Social value: check which model applies

Central government social-value policy is transitioning:

  • PPN 002: Procurement Policy Note 002 (PPN 002) applies to in-scope covered procurements commenced under the Procurement Act 2023 on or after 1 October 2025, subject to its scope and conditions. It requires an appropriate social-value weighting of at least 10% for relevant in-scope procurements.
  • PPN 026: The Cabinet Office has also published PPN 026, a new Social Value Model focused on good jobs and skills. The updated guidance says PPN 026 is to apply to new covered procurements commenced on or after 1 January 2027, where the total contract value is £1 million or more including VAT and the procurement is within scope. It applies to central government departments, executive agencies and non-departmental public bodies; other public authorities may choose to adopt the approach.

For procurements before 1 January 2027, the applicable model may remain PPN 002 or, for earlier procurements and relevant frameworks, PPN 06/20. The date and route of the procurement matter. Suppliers should check the tender documents rather than assume that one model or weighting applies to every opportunity.

The PPN 026 model focuses on outcomes relating to good jobs and skills, including creating high-quality jobs, fair working conditions, fair pay, training and retraining, in-work progression and talent pipelines. Its detailed guidance includes reporting metrics to help authorities assess commitments.

Avoid promising arbitrary local hiring radiuses or fixed local subcontracting percentages unless the procurement documents request them and the commitments are realistic, measurable and deliverable. Strong social-value responses explain who will benefit, what the supplier will do, how outcomes will be measured and how delivery will be monitored.

Carbon Reduction Plans: PPN 006

PPN 006 concerns the use of Carbon Reduction Plans in relevant major central government procurements. The current guidance applies to in-scope central government departments, executive agencies and non-departmental public bodies when awarding relevant public contracts for goods, services or works with an estimated value above £5 million per year, including VAT, under the Procurement Act 2023. The policy is subject to relevance and proportionality and excludes specified special-regime contracts.

The policy also addresses frameworks and dynamic markets where the estimated value of an individual contract to be awarded through them exceeds the stated annual threshold. It does not mean that every public-sector tender above £5 million in total value automatically requires an audited Carbon Reduction Plan.

Where PPN 006 applies, suppliers should prepare a compliant Carbon Reduction Plan in line with the current government guidance and tender requirements. They should accurately describe relevant emissions scopes, the organisation’s baseline and reduction commitments, and the actions planned to achieve them. Do not describe the plan as independently audited unless it has genuinely undergone that assurance.

Practical measures that may be relevant to construction projects include efficient plant and logistics, lower-carbon materials, waste reduction, energy-efficient site facilities and appropriate transport planning. The measures included in a bid should be project-specific and supported by credible delivery evidence.

5. Capacity, skills and delivery readiness

The pipeline’s workforce analysis underlines the scale of labour and skills planning required across the infrastructure sector. Contractors may need to demonstrate that they can secure the people, specialist expertise, equipment and supply-chain capacity needed to deliver work safely and to programme.

Depending on the project, relevant roles may include:

  • Project managers, project directors and construction managers.
  • Quantity surveyors, estimators and commercial managers.
  • Civil, structural, mechanical and electrical engineers.
  • BIM, information-management and digital engineering specialists.
  • Environmental, ecological and geotechnical specialists.
  • Site agents, supervisors, skilled trades and specialist subcontractors.

A credible resourcing response should go beyond an organisation chart and generic CVs. It should identify key personnel, explain their responsibilities and availability, show how resource conflicts will be managed and provide evidence that specialist subcontractors or suppliers can meet the required programme.

Where named personnel or resources are not yet contractually secured, explain the proposed mobilisation and confirmation process honestly. Do not imply guaranteed access to people, equipment or subcontractors unless that commitment is supported by actual arrangements.

6. Illustrative tender response: NEC4 mobilisation and early contractor involvement

Note: Illustrative example only — not a real project or a claim of past performance. It is not a model answer that guarantees a particular evaluation score. The final response must be tailored to the actual contract, scope, programme, evaluation criteria and the bidder’s verified experience.

Example tender question: “Describe your mobilisation strategy for the Early Contractor Involvement phase. Explain how you will identify buildability risks, engage stakeholders and establish operational readiness within the period specified in the tender.”

Suggested response structure

  • Governance and responsibilities: Name the proposed project lead and key workstream owners, define decision-making routes and establish the client communication and reporting rhythm.
  • Contract and programme mobilisation: Review the contract data and scope, confirm the required programme submissions and establish the processes for early warnings, risk reduction meetings and change management where applicable under the selected NEC4 contract.
  • Site information and buildability: Review available surveys, drawings, utility records, access constraints, environmental information and third-party interfaces. Identify information gaps and commission additional surveys only where authorised and necessary.
  • Stakeholder and community engagement: Map affected stakeholders, agree communication responsibilities with the client and set out proportionate arrangements for residents, businesses, statutory undertakers and emergency services.
  • Supply chain and resources: Confirm key subcontractor packages, procurement lead times, competence requirements, plant availability and contingency arrangements. Identify long-lead items and dependencies that could affect the programme.
  • Health, safety and environmental controls: Establish project-specific risk controls, site induction arrangements, environmental management measures and reporting processes before relevant activities begin.
  • Readiness review: Use a documented mobilisation checklist to confirm that people, information, permissions, equipment, facilities and supply-chain arrangements are in place before the relevant work starts.

Evidence to include where available: comparable project references, client-approved performance data, programme extracts, risk registers, staff CVs, training records, supply-chain confirmations and examples of lessons learned. Remove confidential information or obtain permission before using client documents.

The strongest response is specific and verifiable. It explains what the bidder will do, who will do it, when it will happen, how risks will be controlled and what evidence demonstrates that the proposed approach is achievable.

7. Six-point UK infrastructure tender-readiness checklist

  • Opportunity fit: Have you identified the projects, buyers, regions and contract packages that match your capabilities and capacity?
  • Procurement route: Do you understand whether the opportunity is being procured through a framework, dynamic market, project-specific competition or a prime contractor’s supply chain?
  • Compliance evidence: Are your insurance, financial information, health and safety documentation, policies, accreditations and supplier records current and easy to retrieve? Check which credentials are actually required by the tender.
  • Carbon and environmental evidence: Can you provide the Carbon Reduction Plan or other environmental information required by the specific procurement? Make sure claims and emissions data are accurate.
  • Social value evidence: Can you demonstrate measurable employment, skills, training, supply-chain or community outcomes relevant to the contract and the applicable Social Value Model?
  • Bid quality assurance: Is there a structured review process to check compliance, answer the evaluation question, validate evidence, test pricing assumptions and submit before the deadline?

Certifications and schemes such as Constructionline, CHAS/SSIP and ISO management-system standards may be useful or required for particular opportunities, but they are not universal legal prerequisites for every infrastructure tender. Always check the buyer’s stated conditions of participation and contract requirements.

Conclusion: turn pipeline visibility into practical preparation

The £718 billion UK Infrastructure Pipeline provides a significant indication of planned investment and future workforce demand. For contractors, engineers and specialist suppliers, it is a starting point for market research—not a promise of work or a substitute for checking individual procurement notices.

Businesses that understand their target markets, monitor the relevant buyers and frameworks, maintain reliable compliance evidence and prepare project-specific delivery plans will be better placed to assess and respond to suitable opportunities.

Frequently asked questions

What is the UK National Infrastructure Pipeline?

The UK National Infrastructure Pipeline is the government’s forward-looking view of planned major infrastructure projects and investment. The March 2026 update, published by NISTA, identifies 734 planned projects representing £718 billion of public and private investment over the next decade.

How much investment is included in the 2026 UK Infrastructure Pipeline?

NISTA’s 9 March 2026 announcement reports £718 billion of public and private investment across 734 planned projects over the next decade. These are pipeline figures and do not mean that every project is fully funded, approved, procured or guaranteed to proceed.

Does the infrastructure pipeline guarantee upcoming construction contracts?

No. The pipeline provides visibility of planned projects and investment. Individual projects may change in scope, timing, funding or delivery status. Suppliers should monitor the relevant contracting authority and procurement notices for confirmed opportunities.

How are UK infrastructure contracts procured?

Procurement routes vary. They may include framework agreements, dynamic markets, project-specific competitive procedures and subcontracting opportunities through prime contractors. Suppliers should check the procurement documents for each opportunity.

What does the Procurement Act 2023 change for construction tenders?

For procurements under the new regime, the Act uses the Most Advantageous Tender (MAT) concept and requires award criteria to be relevant, clear, measurable, specific and proportionate. Tender documents must explain the assessment methodology and relative importance of the criteria.

What social value requirements apply to infrastructure tenders in 2026?

Requirements depend on the buyer, procurement date, value and scope. PPN 002 applies to relevant in-scope central government procurements commenced from 1 October 2025. PPN 026 is scheduled to apply to new covered procurements from 1 January 2027 where the total contract value is at least £1 million including VAT. Always check the tender documents and applicable policy.

When is a Carbon Reduction Plan required for a public-sector tender?

PPN 006 applies to relevant in-scope central government procurements above £5 million per year including VAT, subject to scope, relevance and proportionality. It does not automatically apply to every public-sector contract above £5 million in total value.

Can SMEs and specialist subcontractors access infrastructure opportunities?

Yes. SMEs may compete directly where eligible, join relevant frameworks or dynamic markets, work in consortia, or pursue subcontracting packages through prime contractors. The best route depends on the project and procurement structure.
